High caseloads are one of the biggest barriers to effective child welfare practice. They prevent staff from building trust and consistent relationships with caregivers and youth. When staff are over-burdened, they can only respond to crises. And caregivers report feeling abandoned.

Work to reduce caseloads. Create conditions for staff to build trusting relationships and provide proactive, consistent support.

## How to do this

**Set maximum caseload standards.** Weight cases by complexity so that workload estimates reflect demand. Advocate for these standards with your legislature and leadership. Saying caseloads need to go down isn't enough. Find and share examples of successful legislative packages or administrative strategies that other states have used to reduce caseloads.

**Block dedicated time for relationship building.** Relationship building can't be treated as something caseworkers do "when they have extra time" because workers don’t have extra time. They're constantly triaging compliance work, tracking down records, driving youth to appointments, and preparing for court and meetings. Structure schedules to protect time for regular contact. Even a short text to let a caregiver know you'll get back to them later builds trust.

**Reduce the transportation and logistics burden on caseworkers.** Caseworkers spend significant time driving to appointments and visits. Increase transportation support through dedicated drivers, ride-share programs, or transit fare so caseworkers can spend that time on relationship building.

**Track how workers actually spend their time.** Monitor time use across caseloads to understand where caseworkers are spending time and where demands are increasing. Use this data to advocate for  caseload reductions and to identify administrative tasks you could reduce or reassign.

## This strategy in action

**Bloom Works** research identified caseload as a significant barrier to kin engagement, caregiver support, and foster caregiver retention.

Sources that report families to child protective services (CPS) may have biases that aren't immediately obvious. For example, they may over-report Black and Brown families, or report families for reasons that don't require child welfare involvement. Hospitals, schools, and other mandated reporters may apply inconsistent standards that result in disproportionate investigations of families of color. If you aren't looking for these patterns, they can persist and harm families.

Analyze referral data to identify bias in referral sources. Use this to provide targeted training and feedback.

## How to do this

**Cross-reference demographic data with referral patterns.** Compare racial and demographic data with screening, testing, and child protection referral numbers.

**Use what you find to target training.** Direct anti-bias training to specific referral sources showing concerning patterns.

**Create standardized practices.** Develop consistent screening and referral practices to reduce subjective decision-making.

**Host listening sessions with grassroots BIPOC-majority family advocates.** Create opportunities for community advocates who work with Black, Indigenous, and families of color to share their experiences with your referral sources and your agency. These sessions help you understand the harm caused by racial and ethnic exclusion in referrals and identify patterns you might not see in data alone. Listen to how referral bias affects families and communities, not just what your internal metrics show.

## This strategy in action

**Washington State** cross-references racial and demographic data with screening, testing, and child protection referral numbers to identify where bias may be happening. This lets them direct anti-bias training to specific referral sources and develop standardized screening and referral practices.

**Bloom Works** research on kin-first approaches found that listening sessions with grassroots advocates revealed patterns of bias and harm that weren't visible in data alone.

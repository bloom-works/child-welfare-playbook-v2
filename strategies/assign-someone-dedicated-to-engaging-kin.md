Though every team member can play a role in maintaining kin connections, without clear ownership over engaging kin, important steps can fall through the cracks.

Formally dedicate someone on your team to be responsible for identifying and connecting with kin. This creates accountability and helps find placements with kin who can serve as lifelong support for youth.

## How to do this

**Figure out where kin engagement fits in your agency.** Determine the right place in your agency for assigning the responsibility of identifying and connecting with kin. A small team may work well when the caseworker is also responsible for engaging kin, while another agency may need a dedicated team focused on this effort. If you're unsure, run a pilot to try out multiple arrangements and get feedback from employees.

**Map out the process for engaging kin and assign responsibilities.** Make sure your process map includes all expected steps. Use your goal metrics to guide the design of your process. Check that each step serves your goals and remove unnecessary steps. Assign responsibilities and resources correctly, and make sure no steps are skipped during transitions. Ask a variety of employees for their feedback. One team may be happy with the current setup, while another team may be frustrated that they regularly find available kin far into a youth's case.

**Make responsibilities official and require teamwork with the caseworker.** Update position descriptions, performance reviews, and other materials to make responsibilities clear. If you have a separate team focused on engaging kin, require them to work closely with the caseworker, the youth, and the parents. Kin identification can't be done in isolation. The caseworker has relationships and context that are essential for finding the right people, and the youth and parents are usually the best source of information about who their trusted adults are.

**Start every kin search by asking family.** Begin all kin searches by asking family members and youth who their trusted adults are. Ask about parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, and anyone else the youth or family identifies as important. If a family is unable or unwilling to share names, look through existing case files, court records, and public records to identify possible connections. With youth, consider using a heart map or other creative tools to help them express who they love and trust. Asking youth and parents first is more likely to surface the kin who actually matter to the youth than database searches alone.

**Give staff the resources and authority to find kin beyond internal databases.** Internal database searches alone won't find all potential kin connections. Staff responsible for identifying and connecting with kin need the ability to work with schools, community members, service providers, and child placing agencies to locate kin. Enable them to contact kin however they can reach them, including through social media, texting, and phone calls. This requires clear authority, resources, and time.

**Set up success measures and empower staff.** Create metrics for success, like the percentage of initial placements with kin or the number of kin identified per case. Use these metrics to guide process design and identify where things get stuck.

## This strategy in action

**Bloom Works** research recommended that all kin searches begin by asking youth and parents who their trusted adults are, rather than starting with database searches. The research found that family-based identification practices don't always carry forward as cases transfer between workers, and that agencies with dedicated kin engagement staff who worked closely with caseworkers and families found more connections than those relying on internal databases alone.

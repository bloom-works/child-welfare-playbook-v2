Youth are often placed with kin quickly during crisis situations. Families need time to process the emotional reality of youth being removed, changing family dynamics, and trauma. Without space for this processing, placements can be unstable as families struggle with grief and adjustment while handling the logistics of caregiving.

Give families emotional space and clinical support during placement transitions so they can process feelings and build stable foundations for care.

## How to do this

**Tell families as early as possible that you plan to place a youth with them.** When possible, discuss plans at shared planning meetings before you remove a youth. Caseworkers are already gathering contacts and aware of parent support networks as early as the investigation stage, so early conversations are often feasible. Don't surprise kin with sudden placements when you can avoid it.

**Schedule longer sessions focused on emotional processing.** Don't limit conversations to logistics like paperwork and schedules. Create dedicated time for youth, parents, and kin to talk about feelings, fears, and hopes. Let families ask questions and express concerns without rushing.

**Provide dedicated clinical services to help families process grief and changes in family dynamics.** Kin may be grieving about child protective services involvement, parents may be grieving the loss of custody, and everyone is adjusting to new family dynamics. Connect families with counselors or therapists who can help them work through these emotions and relationships.

**Communicate clearly about why you're making decisions.** Explain to parents and kin why you removed the youth and how you make placement decisions. Transparency builds trust and helps families understand the process, especially in Indian Child Welfare Act (ICWA) cases where specific legal requirements apply.

## This strategy in action

**Bloom Works** research on kin-first approaches found that quick timelines create severe stress for kin caregivers. Caregivers wanted more help processing the trauma of involvement with child welfare and adjusting to new family dynamics.

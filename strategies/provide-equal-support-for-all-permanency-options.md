Some families and youth would prefer guardianships or other legal arrangements over adoption. But adoptions tend to be better staffed, funded, and understood than guardianships and other permanency options. 

Provide clear explanations of permanency options so families can make informed decisions about guardianship, adoption, or other arrangements. Staff, fund, and explain guardianships on an equal level with adoptions, for both kin and foster caregivers. Involve youth in decisions about their own permanency.

## How to do this

**Provide clear, written explanations of all permanency options.** Create materials that explain guardianship, adoption, and other legal arrangements in plain language. For each option, clearly explain the process, the costs and benefits, and the level of financial support. Staff and court partners often aren’t fully aware of funding differences for guardianships versus adoptions, and they pass this confusion on to caregivers. Make sure foster caregivers understand that guardianship is an option for them.

**Close the staffing and training gaps between guardianship and adoption.** Guardianships offer similar stability to adoptions and may be a better option for some families. Make sure staff are trained and confident in navigating guardianships, not just adoptions. If your agency has a dedicated adoption team but no equivalent guardianship staffing, that gap will affect how well families are served.

**Recognize and honor cultural preferences in permanency planning.** Some communities prefer guardianship over adoption for cultural or religious reasons. Guardianship lets grandparents, aunts, uncles, and other relatives maintain their family roles while parents maintain theirs. Help kin families choose a path based on their values and circumstances. Provide the same financial support  for guardianship and adoption, so families can make decisions based on values instead of financial pressure.

**Involve and educate youth in permanency decisions, especially older youth.** Permanency outcomes versus aging out open different avenues for support with education, finances, and housing. Make sure youth understand their options and have a voice in the process.

**Create transition plans for post-permanency.** Kin caregivers report that once an adoption or guardianship is complete, support disappears. But kin and youth still need support post-permanency. Create transition plans that include personalized check-ins, referrals to outside services, and a clear point of contact for questions. 

**Ensure Indian Child Welfare Act (ICWA) compliance in permanency planning.** Ensure staff are aware of and trained in active efforts to remove barriers to reunification and keep families together. Make sure staff understand that state definitions of kin may not qualify as kin in ICWA cases. Tribes may define kin differently, prioritizing blood relatives or tribal members over the broader definitions some agencies use. Notify tribes early and collaborate on kin-finding, since Tribal agencies can help identify ICWA-compliant placements. Learn from counties where ICWA is being upheld and collaboration with tribes is working well.

## This strategy in action

**Tribal communities in Washington State** tend to favor guardianship over adoption. Recognizing these preferences and making sure financial supports are the same across guardianship and adoption helps families make decisions based on values instead of financial pressure.

**Oklahoma's ICWA Court in Tulsa** is one of the largest in the country with 200 active cases. Tribal child welfare agency workers are notified about cases from the start. They’re often able to accompany state agency workers to removal hearings and stay involved throughout the lifecycle of a case.

**Bloom Works** conducted research on kin-first approaches in Washington State and permanency planning with kin caregivers in Oklahoma. Caregivers consistently reported that they often weren't informed about permanency options, and that guardianship is still seen as less legitimate than adoption by some staff and court partners.
